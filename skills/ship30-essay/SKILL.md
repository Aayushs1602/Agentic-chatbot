# Ship 30 for 30 essay

Write a ~1,250-word essay in the Ship 30 for 30 style, grounded entirely in the
transcript excerpts. The principles are in `reference/principles.md`; the parts
of them that can be checked mechanically are enforced by a rubric after you
write, so following them is cheaper than being corrected.

## The shape

A canonical Atomic Essay is 250 words. At 1,250 the guide's own answer is
**stacked sequences** — repeat the rhythm, don't abandon it. So this is five
Atomic-Essay-shaped sections, not one essay five times the size:

```
# Headline
[Hook — 3-5 sentences, no heading]

## Section 1     ~230 words
## Section 2     ~230 words
## Section 3     ~230 words

## The takeaway  ~150 words
```

## Procedure

**1. Headline.** Clear beats clever. Answer WHO, WHAT and WHY. Prefer
**Credible Names** ("What Madhavan Ramanujam Gets Right About Pricing") or
**The Success Story** — this corpus supplies named operators and real outcomes,
which is exactly what those formats need.

**2. Hook.** Use the lead-in formula: open at the *end* of the story — the
outcome or the surprising result — then drop to its beginning, and make the
reader want the middle. No heading, no throat-clearing, no "In today's fast-paced
world". Land a real claim in the first sentence.

**3. Three body sections.** Each is one idea, in **1/3/1** rhythm: single-sentence
opener, three-sentence development, single-sentence close. Each carries:

- a concrete example from the transcripts, with the guest named;
- one **bolded key sentence** — the section's argument, not a scattered phrase;
- a `[S#]` citation on every factual claim.

Where a section has three or more parallel points, make them a bulleted list
rather than prose. Keep one pattern throughout — don't mix "Step" with "Lesson"
with "Mistake".

**4. The takeaway.** Close with a TL;DR: a compressed bulleted recap, then one
sentence naming what the reader should do differently on Monday. Specific enough
to act on without rereading.

## Rhythm

Alternate short and long sentences. A run of uniformly short sentences reads as
staccato; a run of long ones reads as sludge.

Every sentence pushes the point forward. If a sentence restates the one before
it in different words, cut it.

**The Tequila Test:** if a line could appear in any post on this topic, it is
filler. "Product-market fit is important" fails. "Retention curves that flatten
above zero are the only PMF signal Ramanujam trusts [S2]" passes.

## Grounding

Non-negotiable, and checked after you write:

- Every factual claim carries its `[S#]` marker.
- Never invent a marker — only the provided sources exist.
- Name the guest. "Madhavan Ramanujam found that…" beats "experts say", and it
  is what this corpus uniquely provides.
- If the sources do not support an essay, say so and write nothing. A polished,
  confident, ungrounded essay is the worst possible output here — the polish
  makes it more persuasive, not more true.

## The rubric

After writing, the essay is checked mechanically for: length between 1,000 and
1,500 words; at least three `##` sections; at least one bulleted list; at least
four resolved citations; bold used but not scattered; and no single section
running long. Failures trigger one targeted revision of the offending section
only — so writing to the rubric the first time is faster than being repaired.
