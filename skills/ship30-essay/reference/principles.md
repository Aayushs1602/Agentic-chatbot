# Ship 30 for 30 — writing principles

Extracted from the [Ship 30 for 30 Ultimate Guide](https://www.ship30for30.com/post/how-to-start-writing-online-the-ship-30-for-30-ultimate-guide).
This file is the skill's source of truth. `SKILL.md` is the procedure; this is
the reference it draws on, and `app/agent/ship30.py` turns the checkable parts
of it into a rubric.

**A note on length.** A canonical Atomic Essay is 250 words. The brief asks for
~1,250. The guide's own answer to longer pieces is *stacked sequences* —
"1/3/1 + 1/3/1", repeating the rhythm rather than abandoning it. So this skill
writes five stacked Atomic-Essay-shaped sections, not one essay five times the
size. That is a faithful extension of the method rather than a departure.

---

## 1. Headlines

**Core rule: Clear > Clever.** A headline answers WHO, WHAT and WHY without
making the reader work. A mysterious headline is a failed headline.

The five components (a strong headline uses several, never all):

| Component | What it supplies |
|---|---|
| How Many? | a number — "6 Ways…", "7 Reasons…" |
| WHAT? | the topic itself — non-negotiable |
| WHO? | the named audience, for niche pieces |
| FEEL | emotional tone or context |
| The PROMISE | the outcome the reader gets |

Ten proven formats: BIG Numbers · Dollar Signs · Credible Names · "This Just
Happened" · Question/Answer · The Success Story · Things That Shouldn't Go
Together · For The Industry · Topic Within The Topic · X Number.

For this corpus, **Credible Names** and **The Success Story** are the strongest
plays, because the transcripts supply named operators and real outcomes.

## 2. Structure

Rhythms, expressed as sentences per paragraph:

- **1/3/1** — one-sentence opener, three-sentence development, one-sentence close.
  The default unit.
- **1/5/1** — the same shape with a longer middle.
- **1/2/5/2/1** — crescendo then decrescendo, for a section carrying one big idea.
- **Stacked sequences** — `1/3/1 + 1/3/1`, how the guide scales the form.

Listicle and how-to sections must keep **one consistent pattern**. Do not mix
"Step 1 / Lesson 2 / Mistake 3" — pick one and hold it.

## 3. Sentence rhythm

**Alternate short and long.** Monotony is the failure mode: a run of `1/1/1/1`
reads as staccato, `5/5/5` as sludge. Vary deliberately.

**Rate of revelation.** Every sentence pushes the point forward. If a sentence
restates the previous one in different words, cut it.

## 4. Formatting for skim

- **Wheels & Spokes** — H1 for the piece, H2 for sections, H3 for subsections.
- Any prose paragraph carrying three or more points becomes a bulleted list.
- **Bold the key sentence** of a section, not scattered phrases. Bold is a
  signal; used everywhere it signals nothing.
- Whitespace is structural. Long undifferentiated blocks do not get read.

## 5. Openings

The lead-in formula:

1. Open at the **end of the story** — the outcome, the surprising result.
2. Drop to the **start of the story** — the humble or difficult beginning.
3. Make the reader want the middle.

**The Golden Intersection** — pair actionable advice with a concrete story.
Advice alone is forgettable; story alone is entertainment. For this corpus the
story is a guest's, and it must be cited.

## 6. Closings

Two sanctioned endings:

- **CTA** — point the reader at the next useful thing.
- **TL;DR** — a compressed bulleted recap of what they just read.

Either way the reader leaves with something specific they can act on.

## 7. Do-nots

- Don't be clever at the expense of clear.
- **The Tequila Test** — don't repeat what everyone else already says. If the
  line could appear in any post on the topic, cut it.
- Don't hedge. "It depends" is only acceptable when followed by what it depends on.
- Don't pad to reach a word count. Padding is visible.
- Don't chase perfect.

## 8. What this corpus adds

Two constraints the guide does not have, because this assistant is grounded:

- **Every claim carries its `[S#]` marker.** An uncited assertion is a failure
  even when it is well written — especially when it is well written, because
  polish makes it more persuasive rather than more true.
- **Name the guest.** "Madhavan Ramanujam found that…" is stronger than "experts
  say", and it is what the corpus uniquely provides.
